title: The Conceptual Leap. From Proofs to Reasons
---

I found part a) of Exercise 12 in Chapter 5 very difficult. After much thought, I came up with a solution, but Gemini quickly rejected it because it was wrong. Ultimately, I had to give up. In these cases, what I usually do is add a hint to the exercise. However, this time the solution was so elaborate that I didn't know what hint to offer. After considering the problem for a good while, I concluded that it made much more sense to incorporate this part of the exercise as a theorem within the theory itself.

Part b) was easier, and part c) was fifty-fifty. The first statement to prove didn't present much difficulty, but the second was highly intricate; so much so that I had to go back to Gemini to get unstuck.

At that point, I started analyzing what the chatbot was saying more carefully. After turning it over in my head, drawing diagrams, and factoring arrows, I realized there was a stronger, more general property that implied what Gemini was telling me, and whose proof was much more direct. With that, the solution became less convoluted and, incidentally, gave me the exact material I needed to add that intermediate result as a hint.

Once again, I arrive at the conclusion that elegance is not enough when writing mathematics. We must also be conceptual. That is, a proof that fails to identify the deep, more general reason why our deduction holds might be short and easy to follow, but it will remain merely a proof. Better than proving is giving reasons.

The exact same thing happens in Smalltalk software development: better than programming is assigning responsibilities and discovering knowledge.
